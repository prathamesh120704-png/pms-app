1. What is the difference between a KRA, a KPI and a goal? Give one example of each for the same role. A KRA (Key Result Area) is the broad responsibility area of a role — what the person is mainly accountable for. A KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is the measurable metric used to judge how well that responsibility is being performed. A goal is a specific, time bound target for a particular review cycle. In short: KRA = responsibility, KPI = measurement, goal = cycle-specific commitment. For the same role — Recruitment Manager: • KRA: Improve hiring effectiveness • KPI: Average time-to-fill open positions • Goal: Reduce average time-to-fill from 35 days to 28 days by the end of Q4 This example shows the difference clearly: the KRA says what matters, the KPI says how it is measured, and the goal says what must be achieved in a defined period. 2. What does SMART mean in goal setting, and what makes a badly written goal bad? SMART stands for Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-Bound. A good goal clearly states what will be done, how success will be measured, whether it is realistic, why it matters, and by when it must be completed. A badly written goal is usually vague, task-heavy, unrealistic, or missing a deadline. It may say something like "improve team performance" without defining what improvement means, how it will be measured, or when it should happen. Goals also become weak when they are too numerous and focused on tasks instead of end results. 3. What is a performance appraisal cycle? What are the typical stages, and who acts at each stage? A performance appraisal cycle is the recurring time period in which performance is planned, tracked, reviewed, and finalized. It is not just the final meeting; it is the whole process that connects expectations, progress, feedback, and rating over a defined period such as a quarter or a year. Typical stages: 1. Planning — goals and expectations are set 2. Monitoring — progress is tracked during the cycle 3. Developing — coaching, learning, and support happen 4. Rating/Reviewing — performance is summarized formally 5. Rewarding/Closing — outcomes may feed into recognition, decisions, or next cycle planning In a PMS (as in this project), the typical actors are: • HR Admin — creates the cycle and manages setup • Employee — writes goals and submits self-appraisal • Manager — approves goals and completes manager review • HR Admin — tracks completion and closes the cycle 4. What is a self-appraisal and why do organisations bother asking the employee first? A self-appraisal is a structured reflection in which the employee reviews and often rates their own performance for a given period. It usually includes achievements, challenges, goal progress, and development needs. Organizations ask the employee first because the employee can provide context the manager may not fully see. Self-appraisals help surface hidden work, reduce the feeling of being overlooked, and give managers another perspective to compare against their own assessment. They also make the review conversation more balanced and encourage employee ownership of growth and performance. 5. What is a rating scale? Compare a 3-point, 4-point and 5-point scale. Why do some companies avoid a middle option? A rating scale is the structured scale used to summarize performance in a review, such as "Does Not Meet / Meets / Exceeds" or a numeric 1-to-5 scale. Its purpose is to make evaluations more consistent across employees and managers. • 3-point scale: The simplest — typically Does Not Meet Expectations / Meets Expectations / Exceeds Expectations. Its strength is simplicity and low friction, but its weakness is over-simplification: many employees get lumped into the middle category, making it hard to distinguish different levels of solid performance. • 4-point scale: Usually removes the neutral midpoint. This is considered useful for balancing fairness and accountability because it forces a clearer distinction — for example between "Needs Development," "Meets Expectations," "Exceeds Expectations," and "Sets a New Standard." • 5-point scale: Gives more nuance and is very common, but it can create central tendency bias, where managers overuse the middle rating to avoid difficult judgments. It is more detailed, but only works well when the rating definitions are clear and calibration is strong. Some companies avoid a middle option because they want to reduce centrality bias. If a neutral option exists, managers often drift toward it. Removing it forces a more definite judgment, though it can also feel harsher if the scale is poorly defined. 6. What is normalisation or calibration in appraisals? What is the bell curve and why is it controversial? Calibration or normalisation in appraisals is the process where managers compare and discuss their proposed ratings so that employees across teams are judged using a more consistent standard. The goal is fairness: one strict manager and one lenient manager should not produce wildly different outcomes for equally strong employees. A bell curve is a forced distribution model in which employees are pushed into relative categories such as top, middle, and low performers — for example 20% top, 70% average, 10% low — even if actual performance does not naturally fall that way. It is controversial because it can penalize strong teams, distort reality, and reward politics over evidence. Critics argue that real performance does not always fit a normal distribution, and forced ranking can push strong employees downward simply because too many people performed well. It can also make calibration meetings depend too much on who argues hardest rather than who actually contributed most. 7. What is the difference between OKRs and traditional KRA-based appraisals? OKRs focus on setting ambitious, aligned objectives and measuring progress through specific key results. The emphasis is on strategic alignment, transparency, and forward looking execution. The objective is qualitative; the key results are measurable. Traditional KRA-based appraisals start from role responsibility areas and then assess how well the employee performed within those responsibilities, often through KPIs, manager judgment, and rating scales. They are usually more tied to formal appraisal, role expectations, and performance evaluation. The main difference: OKRs are a goal-setting and alignment framework, while KRA based appraisals are a role-based evaluation framework. In practice, an organization may use OKRs to drive focus during the year and still use a KRA/KPI-based appraisal process for formal review. 8. Name three real PMS products in the market. What data in a PMS is sensitive, and who should never be able to see it? Sensitive PMS data includes employee self-appraisal comments, manager review comments, ratings, overall review summaries, reporting hierarchy, performance history, and any workflow data that may affect appraisal outcomes. In real systems, even goal details may be sensitive depending on the role or function. The most important rule is that employees should never be able to see another employee's private review data just by changing a URL or query parameter. This is treated as a serious access-control issue, requiring role-based access and row-level security thinking. In a basic PMS: • Employees should see only their own review data • Managers should see only their direct reports' relevant review data • HR Admin may see wider process status and records as required by policy • Peers, unrelated managers, and other employees should never see someone else's appraisal comments or ratings
